# What Is a Blockchain?

A **blockchain** is a shared record (a *ledger*) that is copied across thousands of computers around the world. Instead of one company keeping the official database, everyone in the network keeps a copy and they agree on what gets added.

**Web3** is the idea of building apps on top of these shared ledgers, so users can own their assets and data directly, without needing a middleman to hold them.

| | Web2 (traditional) | Web3 (blockchain) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who stores the data? | A company's servers | Many independent computers (nodes) |
| Who can change history? | The company | Practically no one, once confirmed |
| Your account | Username and password, controlled by the company | A wallet and key, controlled by you |
| Transparency | Private database | Public, anyone can read it |

## Blocks and chains

Transactions are grouped into **blocks**. Each block contains:

- A list of transactions
- A timestamp
- The **hash** of the previous block
- Its own hash

Because each block includes the previous block's hash, the blocks are linked into a **chain**.

## What is a hash?

A **hash function** turns any input into a fixed-length "fingerprint". Blockchains like Ethereum and Bitcoin use cryptographic hashes such as Keccak-256 and SHA-256. They have special properties:

- The same input always gives the same hash.
- A tiny change in the input completely changes the hash.
- You cannot work backwards from a hash to the input.

To cheat on a real blockchain, you would have to redo the work for every later block *and* convince most of the network to accept your version. On a large network that is practically impossible.

## Nodes and consensus

Computers that keep a copy of the blockchain are called **nodes**. They must agree on which new block comes next. The rules they use are called a **consensus mechanism**:

- **Proof of Work (PoW)**: "miners" compete to solve a hard puzzle using computing power. Bitcoin uses this.
- **Proof of Stake (PoS)**: "validators" lock up (stake) cryptocurrency as collateral, and can lose it if they cheat. Ethereum has used PoS since 2022 ("The Merge"), cutting its energy use by over 99%.

<Note>
In this track we focus on **Ethereum** and EVM-compatible chains (like Polygon, Arbitrum, Base, and Optimism), since they are the most common platforms for smart contracts.
</Note>

## Public by default

Anyone can read a public blockchain. Block explorers like **Etherscan** let you look up any address, transaction, or contract.

<Warning>
"Public" means everything you do on-chain is visible forever: your balance, your transactions, and who you interacted with. Never put private information on a blockchain.
</Warning>

## Summary

- A blockchain is a shared ledger copied across many nodes.
- Blocks are linked by hashes, so changing old data breaks the chain.
- Nodes agree on new blocks through consensus (Proof of Work or Proof of Stake).
- Public blockchains are transparent: anyone can read every transaction.
